AI Answering for Contractors: Published Prices and the Break-Even Math (2026)

Published prices from two vendors, a break-even formula you can rerun, and the FCC consent rule for AI voices.

In short: Instead of claims, this guide uses published prices: Jobber's AI Receptionist add-on at $99 a month and Smith.ai's human-first plans from $300 for 30 calls. It shows how to compute break-even from your own job value, margin and booking rate.
Contents

Most contractor owners can name the moment a lead slips away: the phone rings while you are under a sink, and by the time you call back, the customer has booked someone else. AI answering tools and live answering services all sell the same fix. This article skips the claims and works from published prices and one formula, so you can see how many recovered jobs it takes for each option to pay for itself. Prices were read from the vendors' pages on November 21, 2026; check them before you buy.

Key points (prices checked November 21, 2026)

  • Jobber lists an AI Receptionist add-on at $99 a month; it answers calls and texts when you are busy, within limits you set.
  • Smith.ai publishes human-first receptionist plans at $300 a month for 30 calls (overage $11.50 per call), $810 for 90 calls ($10.50) and $2,100 for 300 calls ($8.50). Its AI-first and hybrid prices are not listed on that page.
  • Break-even formula: monthly price ÷ expected profit per recovered call. With $80 of expected profit per recovered call, $99 needs 2 recovered calls a month and $300 needs 4.
  • The FCC has ruled that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the TCPA, which requires prior express consent for covered calls.

The break-even formula

The cost of a missed call is not the job value; it is the expected profit. Use these inputs, with your own numbers:

Expected profit per recovered call = average job revenue × gross margin × share of recovered calls you actually book.

An illustrative case, with made-up inputs: $400 average job, 40% gross margin and half of recovered calls booked gives $400 × 0.40 × 0.50 = $80. Then break-even is the monthly price divided by $80:

  • Jobber add-on at $99: 99 ÷ 80 = 1.24, so 2 recovered calls a month.
  • Smith.ai Starter at $300: 300 ÷ 80 = 3.75, so 4 recovered calls a month.
  • Smith.ai Basic at $810: 810 ÷ 80 = 10.1, so 11 recovered calls a month.
  • Smith.ai Pro at $2,100: 2,100 ÷ 80 = 26.25, so 27 recovered calls a month.

"Recovered" means calls you would otherwise have missed that turn into booked jobs. That is the number you do not know yet, and the one to measure before paying for anything. Our missed-call cost calculator lets you enter your own call volume and job value, and the article on the math to run before buying an AI receptionist explains how to count missed calls from your phone records.

Per-call pricing against your call mix

Per-call plans charge for every call answered, including the ones that are not leads: vendors, wrong numbers, spam. Divide the monthly price by the included calls to get the effective price: $10 per call on Starter ($300 ÷ 30), $9 on Basic ($810 ÷ 90) and $7 on Pro ($2,100 ÷ 300). If only 40% of answered calls are real leads and expected profit per lead is $80, expected profit per answered call is $32, well above $10. If only 10% are real leads, it is $8, below the Starter price. Count your last 50 calls by type before choosing a plan with per-call billing.

What to test in a trial

  • Missed-call coverage. Forward your line after three rings and track how many callers leave a booked job versus a voicemail.
  • Wrong answers. Call it yourself and ask about prices, service areas and emergency hours; note anything it gets wrong, because a bad answer costs a customer.
  • Handoff speed. Check how fast a captured request reaches you and in what form (text, email, your scheduling software).
  • Cancellation terms. Confirm whether the plan is month to month before you commit.

The consent rule for AI voices

The FCC ruled in 2024 that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's restrictions on an "artificial or prerecorded voice" apply to current AI technologies that generate human voices, so calls using them require the called party's prior express consent. That matters if a tool places outbound calls or sends automated texts, for instance to follow up on a lead. It is a reason to ask a vendor how it handles consent, and to ask an attorney about your own follow-up messages, before turning on any automated outreach.

What this comparison leaves out

It covers only the prices these vendors publish, and only two vendors, so it is not a market survey. It does not rate call quality, which you can only judge by testing. And the break-even inputs are illustrative: your job value, margin and booking rate will move every result above.
