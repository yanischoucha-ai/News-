Sole Proprietor vs. LLC vs. S-Corp: The Salary Share That Decides Whether the Election Pays (2026 Math)

2026 IRS figures, a model you can rerun for contractors and tradespeople, and the one number that decides whether the S-corp election is worth the paperwork.

In short: A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship, so the LLC alone saves nothing. In our 2026 model the S-corp election beats it only while the owner's salary stays under about 65% of net profit: at $120,000 of profit a 50% salary saves $3,512, a 70% salary loses $893.
Contents

A sole proprietor and a single-member LLC pay exactly the same federal tax, because the IRS ignores the LLC. The real decision is the S-corporation election, and in our 2026 model it only beats sole-proprietor treatment while the owner's salary stays under roughly 65% of net profit: at $120,000 of profit, a 50% salary saves $3,512 a year and a 70% salary loses $893. Calculated November 21, 2026 for a single filer using IRS 2026 figures; this is information, not legal or tax advice.

Key facts as of Nov. 21, 2026
  • Self-employment (SE) tax is 15.3%: 12.4% Social Security on the first $184,500 of 2026 earnings plus 2.9% Medicare with no cap (IRS).
  • For 2027 the Social Security wage base rises to $192,300, announced by the Social Security Administration on October 14, 2026 (Journal of Accountancy).
  • In our model, SE tax costs a sole proprietor 14.1% of net profit at $60,000, $120,000 and $200,000 alike.
  • The S-corp break-even salary is 65.3% of profit at $60,000, 65.9% at $120,000 and 66.2% at $200,000.

Does forming an LLC lower your federal tax?

No. The IRS treats a domestic LLC with one member as an entity disregarded as separate from its owner for income tax, unless it files Form 8832 to be taxed as a corporation (IRS, "Limited liability company (LLC)"). The profit lands on Schedule C, and the owner pays SE tax on it exactly as a sole proprietor would. An LLC buys you a legal shield between the business and your personal assets, which matters for a contractor with crews and vehicles, but it is not a tax move.

The tax lever sits one step further: an LLC or corporation can elect S-corporation status with Form 2553. Shareholders then report the flow-through on their personal returns, and the owner who works in the business must be paid "reasonable compensation" as a W-2 wage before taking any non-wage distribution (IRS, "S corporations" and "S corporation compensation and medical insurance issues"). Only the wage part carries payroll tax. That gap is the whole appeal.

How the model works, so you can rerun it with your numbers

Profit means net profit after all deductible expenses and before any owner pay. Filing status is single, standard deduction $16,100, and the 2026 brackets are 10% to $12,400, 12% to $50,400, 22% to $105,700, 24% to $201,775 and 32% to $256,225 (IRS, Rev. Proc. 2025-32, announced October 9, 2025). No state tax, no other income.

  • Sole proprietor: SE tax = 15.3% × 92.35% of profit, with the 12.4% part capped at $184,500 of earnings. Half of SE tax is deductible. The qualified business income (QBI) deduction takes 20% of QBI, which includes profit minus the deductible half of SE tax, and is capped at 20% of taxable income (IRS, QBI deduction page).
  • S-corp: owner salary W. Payroll tax is 7.65% employee plus 7.65% employer on W. The remaining profit, after salary, employer payroll tax and $2,000 a year of extra payroll and return-preparation costs (our assumption, replace it with your quote), passes through. Salary is not QBI, so the QBI deduction applies only to that remainder.
  • Simplifications: QBI computed without the W-2 wage and property limits that phase in at higher taxable income (see the Form 8995-A instructions; check this above roughly $200,000 of profit); no state unemployment tax, no health-insurance or retirement deductions.

What does the S-corp save at $60,000, $120,000 and $200,000 of profit?

The saving depends almost entirely on what share of profit you can defend as salary. The table shows annual net after federal income tax and payroll or SE tax.

Worked example at $120,000 as a sole proprietor: SE tax is 120,000 × 0.9235 × 15.3% = $16,955. Half of it, $8,477, comes off income. The QBI deduction is capped at 20% of taxable income before it, 20% × ($111,523 − $16,100) = $19,084. Taxable income is about $76,338, income tax $11,506, so net is 120,000 − 16,955 − 11,506 = $91,538. Same profit through an S-corp paying a $60,000 salary: payroll tax $9,180, income tax $13,770, extra costs $2,000, net $95,050.

The S-corp's income tax is higher at every level because salary does not qualify for the QBI deduction. It still wins on payroll tax, until the salary climbs.

Where does the election stop paying?

Solving for the salary where both structures net the same gives 65.3% of profit at $60,000, 65.9% at $120,000 and 66.2% at $200,000. Past that share, the election loses money. The extra $2,000 of admin cost matters most at the bottom: at $4,000 of yearly admin cost and a 50% salary, the result at $60,000 of profit is −$248, at $120,000 it is +$1,864 and at $200,000 it is +$4,433.

So there is no single profit threshold. At a 50% salary and $2,000 of admin cost, the election breaks even near $30,000 of profit (+$26), saves $566 at $40,000 and $1,560 at $60,000, and keeps growing until profit passes about $200,000. At a 70% salary it never wins.

What counts as a defensible salary, and who decides?

The IRS decides if it audits you. It can reclassify distributions as wages, subject to employment taxes, and cites court support from Joly v. Commissioner, Veterinary Surgical Consultants v. Commissioner and David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States (8th Cir. 2012). Its factors include training and experience, duties and responsibilities, time and effort devoted, what comparable businesses pay for similar services and payments to non-shareholder employees.

The key test the IRS names is where gross receipts come from. Receipts produced by non-shareholder employees and by capital and equipment support non-wage distributions. Receipts produced by your own labor must be paid as wages. A contractor who is on the roof every day has an owner-labor share close to 100% of the job revenue she personally generates; one with four crews and trucks has a smaller one. That is why a 40% salary is defensible for the second contractor and a gamble for the first.

The IRS pages we read give no percentage and no safe harbor. Anyone quoting "60/40" as a rule is quoting a habit, not the law. The model's figures are what each salary level costs you, not a recommendation of one.

What changed in the last 60 days

On October 14, 2026 the Social Security Administration set the 2027 wage base at $192,300, up from $184,500 in 2026, along with a 3.6% cost-of-living adjustment. SE tax applies to 92.35% of profit, so a sole proprietor with net profit above roughly $208,000 pays 12.4% on up to $7,800 more earnings in 2027, about $967. Below $200,000 of profit it changes nothing, and none of it affects your 2026 return.

Two older changes still shape the math. The 2026 inflation adjustments, published October 9, 2025, raised the standard deduction to $16,100 for single filers. And for tax years beginning after 2025, the QBI deduction carries a new minimum deduction for active qualified business income, per the IRS page, which the model above does not include.

The decision to make this quarter

If your net profit is under $40,000, stay a sole proprietor or single-member LLC: the S election saves little and the paperwork costs more. If it is above $60,000 and you can document a salary at or below 60% of profit using what a comparable employee would earn, price the election with a CPA who quotes the payroll and return costs, and run the model above with your quote. If your own labor produces most of the revenue, plan on a salary above 65% and expect the election to break even at best.

Limits of the analysis: single filer, federal tax only, no state income or franchise tax, no state unemployment tax on the salary, no retirement or health-insurance deductions, no spouse income. The S-corp saving also lowers the wages counted toward future Social Security benefits, a trade-off the model does not price.

Frequently asked questions

Does an LLC save taxes compared with a sole proprietorship?

No. A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity for federal income tax unless it elects corporate treatment on Form 8832, so profit still goes on Schedule C and bears 15.3% self-employment tax. The LLC adds liability protection, not a tax saving.

When does an S-corp election start to save money?

In our 2026 model, with a 50% salary and $2,000 of added admin cost, it breaks even near $30,000 of net profit, saves $1,560 at $60,000 and $3,512 at $120,000. If salary is 70% of profit, it loses money at every level tested.

How much salary must an S-corp owner pay?

The IRS requires reasonable compensation for services before non-wage distributions and gives no percentage. Its factors include training, duties, time devoted and what comparable businesses pay. Receipts generated by your own labor must be paid as wages.

What is the Social Security wage base for 2026 and 2027?

$184,500 for 2026 (IRS) and $192,300 for 2027, announced by the Social Security Administration on October 14, 2026. The 12.4% Social Security part of self-employment tax applies up to that limit, while the 2.9% Medicare part has no cap.
