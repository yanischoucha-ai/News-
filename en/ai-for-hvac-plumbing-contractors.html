Missed Calls in HVAC and Plumbing: The Math to Run Before You Buy an AI Receptionist

A break-even formula, real pricing from a call-answering vendor, and where the FCC's 2024 AI-voice ruling actually applies.

In short: Invoca data reported by Ainora puts HVAC calls answered by a person at 34%. At a 38% booking rate and a $350 repair, a missed call costs about $133 in expected revenue, so a $300 monthly plan breaks even at 2.3 recovered calls. The FCC's AI-voice ruling covers outbound calls, not answering your own line.
Contents

The answer-rate problem is bigger for HVAC than for plumbing

Invoca's July 2026 benchmark for home services, as summarized by Ainora, reports that 52% of inbound calls across nine home-service trades were answered by a person. Split by trade, only 34% of HVAC calls were answered by a person, against 74% for plumbing. We could not open the Invoca report itself, so treat these as reported figures from a secondary summary and check the original if you quote them.

What happens to a call nobody answers? A CallRail survey of 1,000 US consumers (September 2025, also relayed by Ainora) found 78% of people abandon the business after an unanswered call, and 82% say they would call a competitor. Only 42% said they would leave a voicemail, and that is stated intent, not measured behavior. The same page flags the widely repeated "20-30% of calls missed" claim as an unsourced vendor figure, so we leave it out of the math below.

What a missed call actually costs: the formula to run on your own numbers

Most "you lose $X per missed call" claims multiply missed calls by the full job price. That overstates the loss, because not every caller books. ServiceTitan's June 2022 data across 3,000+ trade businesses (via Ainora) puts the call booking rate at 43% for plumbing, 41% for electrical and 38% for HVAC. Shops with fewer than five technicians booked 24%; shops with 25+ technicians booked 59%.

The formula: weekly loss = missed calls per week × booking rate × average job value. Angi's 2026 cost guides put the average HVAC repair at $350 and the average AC replacement at $5,978 (consumer price guides, not contractor sales data).

Worked example: a small HVAC shop misses 8 calls a week.

  • At a 24% booking rate (small-shop figure): 8 × 0.24 × $350 = $672 per week.
  • At a 38% booking rate (HVAC average): 8 × 0.38 × $350 = $1,064 per week.
  • Over 50 working weeks: $33,600 to $53,200 in repair revenue alone, before any replacement job that starts as a repair call.

That range is a ceiling, not a forecast: it assumes every missed caller would have booked at the average rate. Measure your own missed-call count for two weeks (your phone's call log is enough) before plugging in a number.

What an answering service costs, and the break-even call count

Smith.ai's pricing page (read November 18, 2026) lists, under its human-first receptionist plans: Starter at $300 per month for 30 calls ($11.50 per extra call), Basic at $810 for 90 calls, and Pro at $2,100 for 300 calls, with a 10% discount on 12-month terms. The page does not say which parts of those plans are AI-handled.

Break-even recovered calls per month = monthly cost ÷ (booking rate × job value). On the Starter plan with an HVAC repair: $300 ÷ (0.38 × $350) = 2.3 calls. A small shop at a 24% rate needs 3.6 recovered calls. If the call that gets answered is an AC replacement, one extra booked job pays for several years of the plan: $300 ÷ (0.38 × $5,978) = 0.13 calls.

The example shop above misses about 35 calls a month (8 per week). That is five more than the 30 included in Starter, so it would pay about $58 in overage ($11.50 × 5) for roughly $358 per month. If answering recovers even 3 booked repairs a month ($1,050), the math works. If you miss 3 calls a month, it does not, and a clear voicemail message is the better first step.

The legal trap is on outbound calls, not on answering the phone

In its declaratory ruling FCC 24-17 (adopted February 2, 2024, released February 8), the Federal Communications Commission confirmed that the Telephone Consumer Protection Act's restrictions on "artificial or prerecorded voice" cover AI technologies that generate human voices. Calls using such voices need the called party's prior express consent, and telemarketing calls need prior express written consent. The ruling's language targets outbound calls: it says the requirements apply to AI technology that "initiates any outbound telephone call." It does not address inbound calls answered by AI.

The practical split: an AI that answers your inbound line is outside the scope of that ruling, though state call-recording consent laws can still apply. An AI that dials leads, past customers or old estimates to "follow up" is inside it. The ruling sets no penalty figure itself. This is general information, not legal advice. If you plan to use AI voices for any outbound outreach, ask a lawyer who handles TCPA first.

The decision, by shop size

  • Under five technicians, fewer than 10 missed calls a month: a short voicemail and a same-day callback rule. Cost: nothing. Recovers the callers who would leave a message.
  • 10 to 30 missed calls a month: price an answering option with the break-even formula above. If recovered calls × booking rate × job value exceeds the plan cost by 2x, go ahead.
  • More than 30 missed calls a month, or after-hours emergency work: a live or AI answering service is likely worth trialing; start with a month-to-month term.
  • Any plan to call leads back with an AI voice: stop and get written consent processes in place first.

A voicemail greeting you can use today:

You've reached {Company}. We're on a job right now. Leave your name, address and the problem, and we'll call you back within {X} hours. For a no-heat or no-cooling emergency, press 1 or call {emergency number}.

Track one number for a month: missed calls that were later returned and booked. If that figure is under two, the answering service is not your bottleneck; scheduling and follow-up on estimates probably are.

Frequently asked questions

How many calls do HVAC and plumbing companies answer?

Invoca's July 2026 home-services benchmark, as summarized by Ainora, reports that 52% of inbound calls across nine trades were answered by a person: 34% for HVAC and 74% for plumbing. The original report could not be opened, so verify before quoting.

What is a missed call worth to a contractor?

Expected value = booking rate × job value. With ServiceTitan's reported 38% HVAC booking rate and a $350 average repair from Angi, a missed call is worth about $133 in expected revenue, not the full job price.

Is it legal to use an AI voice to call back leads?

The FCC ruled in February 2024 (FCC 24-17) that AI-generated voices count as artificial voices under the TCPA, so such calls need prior express consent, and telemarketing calls need prior express written consent. This is general information, not legal advice.

Does the FCC ruling cover an AI that answers my inbound calls?

The ruling's language addresses AI technology that initiates outbound calls and does not address inbound calls answered by AI. State call-recording laws may still apply, so check your state's rules.
