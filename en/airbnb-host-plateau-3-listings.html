Going From 2 to 3 Short-Term Rentals: The Time Math and the U.S. Tax Rules That Start to Matter

A time worksheet, the effect of Airbnb's 15.5% host fee, and IRS rules tied to average stay length and personal use.

In short: Workload grows with turnovers, not properties. This article gives a worksheet to price your own time per turnover, shows what a 15.5% host fee does to a three-listing margin, and explains why average stay length and your logged hours can matter for U.S. tax classification.
Contents

The jump from two to three Airbnb or VRBO listings usually feels harder than the jump from one to two, but nobody publishes a reliable "hours per week" figure for it, and a number pulled from thin air would not help you. What you can do is measure your own hours per turnover, price that time, and know which U.S. tax rules begin to depend on those hours. This article gives the worksheet and the rules, with sources checked on November 21, 2026.

Key points (verified November 21, 2026)

  • Airbnb describes a single 15.5% service fee paid by hosts, replacing the old split between guest and host. A software vendor, Smoobu, reports that hosts outside the European Economic Area were migrated on September 15, 2026.
  • Per the IRS (Publication 925), if the average customer stay is 7 days or less, the activity is not a "rental activity" for passive-loss purposes, so the $25,000 rental loss allowance may not be the relevant rule; whether losses are passive then depends on material participation, and one test is more than 500 hours in the year.
  • If you stay in a unit yourself, the IRS "residence" test starts when personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at a fair price (IRS Topic 415).
  • The worksheet below turns your own time log into a monthly cost of manual management, so you can compare it with the price of a co-host or software.

Price your time per turnover, not per week

Workload in short-term rental scales with the number of turnovers, not the number of properties. A listing with 12 stays a month generates twelve rounds of check-in details, cleaner scheduling, review follow-up and the odd mid-stay question. Three listings at 8 stays each generate 24, and each one touches a different calendar and a different cleaner.

Log one month of your real time, in minutes, for each turnover: guest messages before and during the stay, coordinating the cleaner, restocking, checking the clean, pricing changes, and reviews. Then run this formula:

Monthly cost of your time = minutes per turnover × turnovers per month ÷ 60 × the hourly value you put on your time.

An illustrative case, with made-up inputs you should replace: 40 minutes per turnover, 24 turnovers a month and a $40 hourly value give 40 × 24 ÷ 60 × 40 = $640 a month. If a co-host or software costs less than that for the same work, the math favors delegating; if it costs more, it does not. The result depends entirely on your minutes, which is why the worksheet matters more than any benchmark.

What the 15.5% host fee does to a three-listing P&L

Airbnb describes a single 15.5% service fee paid by hosts instead of splitting the fee between guest and host. Smoobu reports that, in the earlier model, hosts paid about 3% of the booking subtotal and guests paid 12% to 14% on average. Airbnb's own page does not state the old host rate; its example implies roughly 3%.

Take three listings that each book $3,000 a month, $9,000 in total. At about 3% the host fee would be around $270 a month; at 15.5% it is $1,395, a difference of about $1,125 a month, or $13,500 a year, if your nightly prices did not change. Airbnb's page notes that hosts who did not adjust prices see lower payouts, while guests still see the same total in its example. The practical step is to check one payout statement for the actual percentage taken and rebuild your net margin from it, rather than relying on last year's spreadsheet.

Why average stay length matters for taxes

IRS Publication 925 lists exceptions to the "rental activity" definition. The first one is that the average period of customer use is 7 days or less; you compute it by dividing the total days of all rental periods by the number of rentals in the year. When it applies, the activity is not a rental activity for the passive activity rules, and it can be treated as a trade or business that is passive only if you do not materially participate. One of the participation tests in that publication is working more than 500 hours in the activity during the year.

Two consequences follow, and neither requires a view on your specific return. First, the familiar $25,000 allowance for rental real estate losses, which phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income, applies to passive rental real estate in which you actively participate; for stays averaging 7 days or less it may not be the rule that governs you. Second, your hours log is no longer only a management tool: it can be the evidence for how your activity is classified. Keep dated records of what you did, rather than a year-end estimate. Which classification applies to you, and what it means for your losses, is a question for a CPA.

If you stay in the unit yourself

IRS Topic 415 says a dwelling unit also counts as your residence if your personal use exceeds the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days you rent it at a fair rental price. Personal use includes days used by you, by family members, and by anyone paying less than a fair price. Once the unit counts as a residence, rental expense deductions are limited, and expenses are generally divided between rental and personal use based on days. If you use a unit as a residence and rent it for fewer than 15 days in the year, you do not report the income or deduct the expenses.

With three listings the risk is in the small stays: a long weekend for your family, a free night for a friend, a stay at below-market price. Log them as personal days when they occur.

A decision checklist before listing number three

  • Measure first. One month of minutes per turnover, from your own messages and calendar.
  • Rebuild the margin. Use the fee percentage shown on your latest payout, not the one in your old model.
  • Check the average stay. Total rental days divided by number of rentals, per listing and overall, before assuming which tax rule applies.
  • Price the alternatives. Compare your monthly time cost with a co-host quote or software subscription for the same tasks; the answer can differ for each property.
  • Test the downside. If occupancy falls 20%, does the third listing still cover its fixed costs?

What this analysis leaves out

It does not cover state and local rules, which vary by city: registration, permits, lodging taxes and caps on nights. It does not decide your federal classification, which depends on the services you provide and your hours. It also uses an illustrative time cost; your minutes per turnover will differ, and they are the number that drives the decision.

Frequently asked questions

How do I estimate the time cost of managing three listings?

Log your minutes per turnover for a month, multiply by turnovers per month, divide by 60 and multiply by the hourly value of your time. The result is your monthly cost of manual management.

What fee does Airbnb charge hosts now?

Airbnb describes a single service fee of 15.5% paid by hosts. Check a recent payout statement for the percentage actually deducted on your listings.

Why does average stay length matter for taxes?

Per IRS Publication 925, if the average customer use is 7 days or less the activity is not a rental activity for passive-loss purposes, so classification can depend on material participation instead.

When does a rental unit I also use count as my residence?

Per IRS Topic 415, when your personal-use days exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of the days rented at a fair price. Rental expense deductions are then limited.
