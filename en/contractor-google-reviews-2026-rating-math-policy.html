Contractor Google Reviews in 2026: The Rating Math and What Google Now Removes

A rating-and-recency formula you can run on your own profile, the review requests Google's written policy rules out, and the Business Profile changes since late September.

In short: Consumers filter on rating and recency before they call: 47% skip businesses under 20 reviews and 74% only care about the last three months (BrightLocal, 1,002 US adults, Feb. 2026). A 4.3 profile with 40 reviews needs 16 five-star reviews to reach 4.5. Google bars incentives, staff quotas and requests for specific content, and its Business Profile rules changed between Sept. 23 and Oct. 6, 2026.
Contents
Key points (checked Nov. 19, 2026)
  • BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults found that 47% won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews, 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months, and 31% will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher.
  • A contractor at 4.3 stars with 40 reviews needs 16 new five-star reviews to reach a 4.5 average (formula below).
  • Google's content policy bars incentives of any kind for reviews, and bars asking staff to hit review quotas or asking customers to include specific content such as a technician's name.
  • Between Sept. 23 and Oct. 6, 2026, Google Business Profile changed its suggested-edit window, Maps review access and listing rules, according to trade press (details and evidence level below).

What do homeowners actually do with your Google reviews before they call?

They filter on rating and recency before they read a word. BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (published Feb. 11, 2026; a panel of 1,002 US adults surveyed through SurveyMonkey) found that 31% of consumers will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, 47% won't use one with fewer than 20 reviews, 74% only care about reviews written in the last three months, and 32% look for reviews from the last two weeks, up from 20% the year before.

Two cautions. This is a general local-business survey, not a contractor study, and BrightLocal sells local SEO software, so it has an interest in reviews mattering. And a stated preference is not a measured booking rate. But the direction is consistent with what a homeowner does at 9 p.m. with a leaking water heater: opens Maps, sorts by rating, checks that someone reviewed a job this season, and calls the first plausible name.

The practical reading: an old five-star profile is weaker than it looks. A contractor with 80 reviews and none since spring fails the recency test that three out of four consumers say they apply.

How many reviews do you need to move from 4.3 to 4.5?

The number of new five-star reviews needed is: current review count × (target average − current average) ÷ (5 − target average). Take a contractor with 40 reviews averaging 4.3. To reach 4.5: 40 × (4.5 − 4.3) ÷ (5 − 4.5) = 16. Check: (4.3 × 40 + 5 × 16) ÷ 56 = 4.5 (computed in Python for this article).

Now the time cost. Reviews per month = completed jobs per month × share of customers you ask × share who actually post. Say 12 jobs a month, every customer asked, and 20% post a review (an assumption to replace with your own rate, because BrightLocal's figures don't give you yours). That's 2.4 reviews a month, so 16 reviews take about 6.7 months. At a 30% response rate it's 4.4 months; at 15%, 8.9 months.

Two things follow. First, the response rate is the lever you control, and it depends almost entirely on when and how you ask: a text with a direct link within an hour of finishing the job, not a card left in the truck. Second, a steady trickle beats a burst. Google's policy lists "unusual volumes or patterns of review contributions" as rating manipulation, so 16 reviews in one week from a push is the wrong way to close the gap.

What can you say when you ask a customer for a review?

You can ask for an honest review, with a direct link, without offering anything and without telling them what to write. Google's Maps and Business Profile content policy (read Nov. 18, 2026) bars merchants from offering incentives "such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" for reviews, from discouraging negative reviews or selectively soliciting positive ones, and from requiring or pressuring customers to review while on the premises. It also says merchants should not "request that specific content be included," and gives two examples: asking staff to solicit a certain number of reviews, and asking for reviews that identify a staff member.

That last line hits a common contractor habit. If you pay technicians a bonus per review, or ask customers to "mention Mike," you are on the wrong side of the written policy. SearchLab Digital, a local-search agency, reported on March 31, 2026 that it had seen reviews disappear in the wild for these reasons; that is an observation from one agency, not a Google statement.

The federal rule is different and narrower. The FTC's Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule Q&A says the rule does not prohibit giving incentives for reviews, as long as there is no express or implied requirement that the review express a particular sentiment, and that failing to disclose incentives could still violate the FTC Act. So a discount for a review can be lawful under that rule and still get your reviews removed by Google. Follow the stricter of the two: no incentives.

A message you can copy: "Thanks for choosing us for the [job]. If you have two minutes, an honest review helps other homeowners find us: [link]. Whatever you write is fine, including if something fell short." Send it by text, the same day.

What changed on Google Business Profile in the last 60 days?

Three things, with different levels of evidence. According to MarketPros.AI's roundup of Oct. 6, 2026, which cites Search Engine Roundtable and Google help pages: on Sept. 23 Google's help page began stating that owners have four days to accept or reject a suggested edit before Google may publish it; on Sept. 30 Maps began requiring a sign-in to read all reviews, sort them and see all photos (observed in a prompt, with no explanation from Google, and the scope and regions unknown); and on Oct. 6 the content policy was updated so that listings may not contain time-limited promotions, prices or calls to action, while posts may not contain email addresses, phone numbers, social links or unrelated links.

What to do this week, in order. Log in and clear any pending suggested edits. Send Business Profile notifications to an inbox someone reads within a day or two. Remove "Call now for 20% off" from your business description and any phone number from the text of your posts; the phone number belongs in the contact field. Then make sure your website shows the same hours, address and services as the profile, because the roundup notes that Google may publish an edit when public information such as your site supports it.

The sign-in change cuts both ways and is still unexplained, so watch it rather than build a plan on it. If a logged-out visitor sees only part of your reviews, the reviews that show first carry more weight, which is one more reason recency matters.

Why should the proof also live on your own website?

Because you control it, and it does not depend on a visitor's sign-in or on a Google removal. The same BrightLocal survey found ChatGPT and other generative AI tools used for local business recommendations by 45% of respondents, up from 6% the year before, which means assistants are also reading what your own pages say about your work.

A project page with the job, the before-and-after photos, the city, the price range and a customer quote the customer agreed to publish is proof that stays put. Should you chase more reviews or fix the profile first?

Fix the profile first if any of these is true: pending suggested edits, hours that differ from your website, a phone number or a promotion in your listing text, or technicians paid per review. These cost nothing and can cost you reviews or listing accuracy.

Then run the review math with your own numbers. If you are under 20 reviews, getting to 20 is the first goal, because 47% in the BrightLocal panel say they won't use a business below that. If you are above 20 but under 4.5, use the formula. If you have both rating and count but nothing in the last three months, the job is cadence: one ask per completed job, every job, same day.

The limit of this analysis: every consumer figure here is stated preference from a US panel, and we found no published study that measures the effect of review recency or rating on contractor bookings specifically. Track your own calls by source for 90 days before and after you change the process.

Frequently asked questions

How many Google reviews does a contractor need?

In BrightLocal's 2026 survey of 1,002 US adults, 47% said they won't use a business with fewer than 20 reviews. Twenty is a floor, not a target, and 74% also say they only care about reviews written in the last three months.

Can I offer a discount for a Google review?

Not on Google. Its content policy bars merchants from offering incentives such as payment, discounts or free goods or services for reviews. The FTC rule is narrower and allows incentives that are not conditioned on a particular sentiment, but Google's stricter rule is the one that gets reviews removed.

Can I ask customers to mention a technician by name in a review?

No. Google's policy says merchants should not request that specific content be included, and gives asking for reviews that identify a staff member as an example. It also lists asking staff to solicit a certain number of reviews.

How many 5-star reviews does it take to raise a 4.3 average to 4.5?

With 40 existing reviews, 16 new five-star reviews. The formula is current count × (target − current average) ÷ (5 − target). At 12 jobs a month and a 20% response rate, that takes about 6.7 months.
