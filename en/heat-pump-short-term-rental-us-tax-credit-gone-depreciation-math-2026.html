Heat Pump for a US Short-Term Rental: The Federal Credit Is Gone, and the Payback Math Depends on Your Electric Rate

The $2,000 credit ended on Dec. 31, 2025 and excluded landlords even before. What remains is depreciation, state rebates and your electric rate.

In short: A heat pump in a US short-term rental earns no federal credit in 2026. The deduction is slow (27.5 years), so payback rests on energy savings: about 14.7 years at the U.S. average rate for a $12,000 system replacing electric resistance, 5.5 years if you must replace the old equipment anyway.
Contents

Key points (checked Nov. 21, 2026)

  • The federal Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit (Section 25C, up to $2,000 for a heat pump) is not allowed for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to IRS fact sheet FS-2025-05 (Aug. 21, 2025). Even before that date, the IRS page states that you cannot claim it as a landlord who does not live in the home.
  • A heat pump in a rental is a capital improvement. Under IRS Publication 527 (2025), a furnace added to a residential rental is depreciated over 27.5 years, so a $12,000 system yields about $436 of deduction a year.
  • With 9,000 kWh of electric-resistance heating a year at the U.S. average of 18.16 cents per kWh (EIA, August 2026), halving electricity use saves about $817 a year: a 14.7-year simple payback, 16.5 years after tax at the 24% bracket.
  • The decision that actually moves payback is whether you must replace the old equipment anyway. In that case the same system pays back in 5.5 years on its extra cost.

Is there still a federal tax credit for a heat pump in a short-term rental?

No. The Section 25C credit that paid up to $2,000 a year for a qualified heat pump is not allowed for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per IRS fact sheet FS-2025-05, which implements the July 4, 2025 law known as the One, Big, Beautiful Bill. The same fact sheet ends the residential clean energy credit (25D, solar and geothermal for homeowners) for expenditures made after that date, and the new energy efficient home credit (45L) for homes acquired after June 30, 2026.

For a host, the more important detail is older than the repeal. The IRS credit page says that you can't claim the credit if you're a landlord or other property owner who doesn't live in the home. A listing you rent to guests never qualified, even in 2024. If an installer or a blog post still promises you a $2,000 credit on a rental, the claim is wrong twice.

The commercial-building deduction under Section 179D has a similar clock: no deduction for property whose construction begins after June 30, 2026. It targets commercial buildings and large multifamily, not a three-bedroom house, so it rarely applies to a host.

What can a host actually deduct for a heat pump?

You deduct the cost over time, not in one year. Publication 527 (2025) defines residential rental property as a building where 80% or more of gross rental income comes from dwelling units, and it uses a furnace as its own worked example: a $4,000 furnace placed in service in May is depreciated as residential rental property at 2.273% in the first year, which is $91. The straight-line period is 27.5 years and the mid-month convention applies.

Apply that to a $12,000 heat pump installed in January: 3.485% in year one is $418, then $436 a year (12,000 / 27.5). Installed in December, the first year drops to 0.152%, about $18. The calendar matters little: the total deduction is the same, only the start moves.

Two doors that look attractive are mostly closed. Bonus depreciation became permanent at 100% for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025 (IRS Notice 2026-11, issued Jan. 14, 2026, as summarized by CBIZ), but it applies to property with a recovery period of 20 years or less, and a heat pump in a residential rental falls in the 27.5-year class unless a cost-segregation study separates a component into a shorter class. Section 179 expensing for HVAC is limited to improvements to the interior of nonresidential buildings (Publication 946, 2025). A host with a house is outside that rule.

One open question is yours to settle with a tax preparer. Publication 527 excludes a unit in a hotel, motel, inn or other establishment where more than half of the units are used on a transient basis. If your short-let is classed that way, the recovery period is longer and the yearly deduction shrinks: $12,000 over 39 years is $308 a year instead of $436. The sources do not settle where a single-house host lands, so do not assume.

How long does a heat pump take to pay back in a rental?

Start from energy, because it is the only recurring benefit. The Department of Energy states that modern air-source heat pumps can reduce your electricity use by 50% compared to furnaces and baseboard heaters (Energy.gov, page updated May 14, 2026). That figure applies when you replace electric resistance. If you currently burn propane, oil or gas, the savings are a fuel-price comparison and you need your own numbers.

The worked example uses these inputs. The first one comes from EIA's Electric Power Monthly: the U.S. average residential price was 18.16 cents per kWh in August 2026, against 17.57 cents a year earlier. The other two are assumptions to replace with your own: 9,000 kWh a year of heating electricity (a 1,400 square foot unit with baseboard or electric furnace heat) and a $12,000 installed price.

  1. Current heating bill: 9,000 kWh × $0.1816 = $1,634 a year.
  2. Savings at 50%: $817 a year.
  3. Simple payback: $12,000 / $817 = 14.7 years.
  4. After tax at 24%: you keep 76% of the savings ($621) because the electricity expense you no longer pay was deductible, and you gain $436 × 24% = $105 of tax saving from depreciation. Net $726 a year, payback 16.5 years.

At the 12% bracket the payback is 15.6 years; at 32%, 17.3 years. Tax changes the answer by about two years, not by a different order of magnitude, which is why the lost credit hurts less on a rental than a headline suggests: the credit never applied to you.

Where does the electric rate flip the verdict?

The same system on the same 9,000 kWh gives a very different result depending on the state. EIA's August 2026 residential prices put it in the table below. The simple payback uses the 50% saving and the $12,000 assumption.

The pattern holds: where electricity is expensive, replacing resistance heat pays; where it is cheap and winters are mild, a heat pump bought only for savings does not.

Why the replacement case changes the answer more than any tax rule

A unit that is failing has a baseline cost. If a like-for-like replacement (a new electric furnace and a separate AC) would cost $7,500, only the $4,500 difference needs to be earned back by the heat pump. Against the $817 saving that is 5.5 years, or 6.8 years after tax with depreciation on the extra amount. The $7,500 assumption is an example; get both quotes in writing from the same contractor, then use the formula: (heat pump price − like-for-like price − rebates) / annual saving.

Guests add a second benefit that is hard to price: a unit that heats and cools quietly. Whether that lifts your nightly rate or reviews is something your own listing data can answer, not this article. Do not add it to the payback until you have measured it.

What changed in the last 60 days for state rebates?

The Department of Energy's Home Energy Rebates (HOMES up to $8,000, HEEHR up to $14,000, per energy.gov, last updated July 8, 2026) are the main remaining federal money, paid through state programs. As of mid-October 2026, only West Virginia and Florida had federal approval for revised programs, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence (Oct. 15, 2026). DOE approved Florida's HEEHR program on Oct. 14; West Virginia reported more than 1,000 applications in two weeks. Oregon was enrolling contractors without a launch date, Montana did not expect rebates this winter, and Illinois, Kentucky, North Dakota and Texas were on hold.

The revised guidance also bars using the rebates to replace appliances running on oil and natural gas with electric ones, though a home may keep its fossil-fuel system alongside a new heat pump. Whether a short-term rental counts as an eligible household varies by state program rules, which this article could not confirm for any state: check your state energy office before you sign a quote, because buying first can disqualify the project.

Practical order for this week: get your last 12 months of electric bills and add the heating months; ask two contractors for a heat pump quote and a like-for-like quote; check your state energy office for a live rebate and whether it accepts rental units; then ask your tax preparer which recovery period applies to your property. Do not sign anything that assumes a federal credit.

What this analysis does not cover

The 50% saving is a DOE general figure; actual savings depend on climate, sizing, ductwork and thermostat settings, and cold-climate performance varies by model. The installed price is an assumption and real quotes in 2026 vary widely by region and by whether ducts exist. Vacancy matters too: if your unit sits empty with the heat set low, the savings come from the nights you host. This is information, not tax advice; the tax points were checked against IRS pages on Nov. 21, 2026 and rules can change, so confirm with a qualified preparer before filing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I claim the heat pump tax credit on a short-term rental in 2026?

No. The Section 25C credit is not allowed for property placed in service after Dec. 31, 2025, and the IRS says landlords who do not live in the home could not claim it even before that date.

How is a heat pump depreciated in a rental property?

Publication 527 treats a furnace added to residential rental property as part of that property, depreciated straight-line over 27.5 years. A $12,000 system gives about $436 a year, or $418 in the first year if installed in January.

How long does a heat pump take to pay back in a rental?

At the U.S. average 18.16 cents per kWh and 9,000 kWh of resistance heating, a $12,000 system saves about $817 a year: 14.7 years simple payback, 16.5 years after tax at 24%. If you must replace failed equipment anyway, the extra cost pays back in about 5.5 years.

Are DOE home energy rebates available for rental properties in 2026?

Only West Virginia and Florida had federal approval for revised programs as of mid-October 2026 (S&P Global). Whether a short-term rental qualifies depends on each state's rules, so check your state energy office before buying.
