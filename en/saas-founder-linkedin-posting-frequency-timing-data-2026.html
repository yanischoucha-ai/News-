SaaS Founder LinkedIn Posting Frequency: What the Data Says

More posts raise engagement rate but shrink reach per post. Here is what Buffer's own regression table shows, and what an hour of your time buys at each cadence.

In short: Two to five LinkedIn posts a week is the range best supported by Buffer's data: engagement rate rises with frequency while reach per post slips. Timing matters less than cadence, and the trade-off can be calculated in impressions per hour of founder time.
Contents

Key facts (checked November 22, 2026)

  • In Buffer's analysis of about 2 million LinkedIn posts from 94,485 accounts (published August 2025), weeks with 2-5 posts had an engagement rate 0.23 percentage points above the same account's 1-post weeks; 6-10 posts, 0.76 points; 11 or more, 1.40 points.
  • The same regression shows reach per post falling by 41.6 impressions (2-5 posts), 184.0 (6-10) and 650.2 (11 or more) against 1-post weeks.
  • Buffer's September 9, 2026 study of 4.8 million LinkedIn posts puts the strongest window at 3 p.m. to 8 p.m. on weekdays, with Wednesday at 4 p.m. on top and Monday and Tuesday lowest.

A SaaS founder with limited time should post 2 to 5 times a week on LinkedIn, and on that range the data points one way: each post gets a bit less reach, but the account earns a higher engagement rate and far more total reach than at one post a week. Posting more than 5 times keeps raising the engagement rate and total reach, yet each post reaches fewer people, so the return per hour of work starts to slip.

How many LinkedIn posts a week should a SaaS founder publish?

Start at three. It is the middle of the 2-5 range, it fits in about 90 minutes if a post takes 30 minutes, and it is the cadence where Buffer's data shows a clear gain with almost no loss. None of the studies below isolates software companies, and none isolates US accounts: Buffer's samples come from accounts connected to its scheduling tool worldwide, Socialinsider's from company pages. Treat every number as LinkedIn in general.

The one finding that holds across Buffer's analyses is the cost of silence. In its March 5, 2026 report on 52 million posts, Buffer found that accounts skipping a week underperformed their own follower-growth baseline, and that even 1-2 posts in a week beat none. That test covered Facebook, Instagram and X, not LinkedIn, so for LinkedIn it is a reasonable bet, not a measurement.

What does Buffer's regression table show that its summary does not?

Buffer's August 28, 2025 summary article says posting more brings "+1,182 more impressions per post" at 2-5 posts a week and concludes you cannot post too much. The data scientist's own page, dated August 1, 2025, publishes the regression output, and the reach coefficients are negative. Here is that output, with each account compared to its own 1-post weeks:

Buffer's March 2026 report agrees with the table, not the summary: "reach per post tends to decline at higher frequencies." We cannot tell why the summary article diverges, so the table and the 2026 report are what to rely on.

Two limits matter for a founder. First, the model only keeps posts with more than 5 engagements, which removes the weakest posts and flatters every tier. Second, frequency explains almost none of the week-to-week variation: the within-account R-squared is 0.0002 for engagement rate and 0.00003 for reach. Cadence moves the average; it does not decide any single week.

Does posting time matter more than frequency?

No, according to Buffer itself, and the gap between slots is modest. Its March 2026 report ranks the levers in this order: what you post matters most, how often matters a lot, when matters least. The September 9, 2026 timing study, which covers 4.8 million LinkedIn posts, still gives a usable starting point: weekdays from 3 p.m. to 8 p.m., Wednesday at 4 p.m. first, then Friday at 3 p.m. and 4 p.m. Morning posts (6 a.m. to 11 a.m.) did worse than afternoon and evening ones, which shifts the 2025 pattern, when peaks sat inside working hours.

The same study notes that LinkedIn's own marketing blog cites research where Tuesday is among the most active days, so Monday and Tuesday weakness is a finding about Buffer's dataset, not a law. Buffer also normalizes times to local time, which tells you the clock reading, not where your buyers are.

A decision rule for your own audience, not a statistical test: for 8 weeks, post on a Wednesday afternoon slot in even weeks and a Tuesday morning slot in odd weeks, same format. After 8 weeks you have 4 posts per slot. Compare the median engagements per post; move your default only if the new slot wins in at least 3 of the 4 pairs. With only 4 pairs this is a rough signal, and you should repeat it before restructuring your week.

What is the return per hour of founder time at each cadence?

Use this formula: weekly reach = posts per week × (baseline reach per post + change for that tier), and reach per hour = weekly reach ÷ (posts × minutes per post ÷ 60). Take Buffer's mean reach per post in 1-post weeks, 694 impressions, and an assumed 30 minutes per post (an example, replace it with your own time and your own LinkedIn analytics average):

  1. 1 post: 694 × 1 = 694 impressions in 0.5 hour, or 1,388 per hour.
  2. 3 posts: (694 - 41.6) × 3 = 1,957 impressions in 1.5 hours, or 1,305 per hour. That is 2.8 times the reach of one post for 3 times the work.
  3. 8 posts: (694 - 184.0) × 8 = 4,080 impressions in 4 hours, or 1,020 per hour. That is 5.9 times the reach for 8 times the work, and 27% less reach per hour than one post.

We left out the 11-or-more tier on purpose: its 260.8 standard error is large compared with the 694 baseline, and the baseline comes from a different population than the accounts posting that often. The example also mixes a cross-account mean (694; the median is 264) with within-account changes, so it shows the shape of the trade-off, not your numbers. On those inputs, three posts costs you 6% of reach per hour and eight costs 27%: the extra posts still add reach, but each hour buys less.

What changed on LinkedIn in the last 60 days?

On November 9, 2026, LinkedIn's engineering blog described "Profile Relevance," a system that uses large language models to match a member's profile (headline, skills, work history, education) with posts from outside their network, according to the agency Influent's November 14 recap. We could not open LinkedIn's page, so this rests on that summary. The practical reading, which is an editorial inference and not a LinkedIn rule: a post aimed at a role and a problem ("a VP of Support trying to cut first-response time") gives the system more to match than a post about "the future of customer support."

Socialinsider updated its LinkedIn benchmarks on November 6, 2026 with third-quarter rates: native documents and multi-image posts 6.40%, video 5.25%, images 4.70%, text 3.50%, links 2.50%. Links in the post body were the weakest format in both its 2025 year (3.25%) and Q3 2026. If your posts carry a link to the blog in the first line, move it to a comment for one of your three weekly posts and compare after four weeks.

The earlier shift, from March 2026, was LinkedIn's announcement that it would reduce engagement bait ("comment Yes if you agree") and recycled thought leadership, reported by Social Media Today on March 12, 2026. It is older than 60 days, but it is the reason a bait-style post is a worse use of your 30 minutes now.

What should you do with 30 minutes, 90 minutes or 4 hours a week?

Thirty minutes a week buys two short text posts of 15 minutes each; skip documents and video at this budget. Ninety minutes buys three posts plus nothing else, or two posts plus 30 minutes of replies: Buffer found that LinkedIn accounts replying to comments averaged 30% more engagement (about 2 million posts across six networks; 83% of LinkedIn profiles did better when they replied, a correlation, not proof). Four hours is where a monthly document post (carousel PDF) starts to make sense, since it is the best-performing format in both Buffer's and Socialinsider's data, but do not push past five posts a week to fill the time.

For choosing topics, a spreadsheet is enough: list 10 customer questions you answered by email this month and one product metric you can disclose. If you want a prompt for a topic mix, a free strategy analysis for SaaS sites is available from a social content strategy analysis for SaaS (about two minutes, no card or account; a tool from the same company that publishes this site).

The advice fails in two cases. If your buyers are not on LinkedIn (developer tools sold through GitHub, for example), none of this data applies. And if 3 posts a week would make each one generic, drop to 2: Buffer's own author says low-quality content posted often does not perform. What remains uncertain is how Profile Relevance changes reach per post, which no open dataset measures yet.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a SaaS founder post on LinkedIn?

Two to five times a week, based on Buffer's analysis of about 2 million LinkedIn posts: that range raised engagement rate by 0.23 points per post over 1-post weeks while reach per post fell by only 41.6 impressions. No study cited here isolates software companies.

Does posting more often reduce reach on LinkedIn?

Per post, yes: in Buffer's regression reach per post fell by 41.6, 184.0 and 650.2 impressions at 2-5, 6-10 and 11 or more posts a week. Total reach still rose, and the engagement rate rose too, so the loss is in efficiency per post.

What is the best time to post on LinkedIn in 2026?

Buffer's September 9, 2026 study of 4.8 million posts found 3 p.m. to 8 p.m. on weekdays strongest, with Wednesday at 4 p.m. first. Buffer says its own March 2026 data ranks timing as the least important of the three levers, so test your audience before changing your schedule.

Which LinkedIn format has the highest engagement rate?

Native documents (PDF carousels): 7.00% in 2025 and 6.40% in Q3 2026 according to Socialinsider, against 4.50% and 3.50% for text posts. Posts with links had the lowest rates, 3.25% in 2025 and 2.50% in Q3 2026.
