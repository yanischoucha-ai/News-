Trade Pricing 2026: Set an Hourly Rate That Holds Margin

Loaded labor, utilization, overhead and the lost-job threshold, with a worked three-tech example and fall 2026 cost data.

In short: In a three-tech example, a 15% net margin requires $125.08 per billable hour. At a 40% contribution margin, a 5% price increase holds contribution dollars unless more than 11.1% of jobs disappear.
Contents

To price trade work without bleeding margin, divide your loaded labor cost by the share of paid hours you can bill, add overhead per billable hour, then divide by 1 minus your target margin; in our three-tech example that comes to $125.08 per billable hour. Raise it 5% and, at a 40% contribution margin, you can lose up to 11.1% of your jobs before contribution dollars fall.

Plenty of owners are moving. In October 2026, a net 37% of small business owners planned to raise prices in the next three months, the highest reading since July 2022, according to NFIB. That survey covers NFIB members across all industries, not only contractors.

Key points (checked November 26, 2026)

  • Prices for building materials used in homebuilding were up 5.8% year over year in October 2026, and No. 2 diesel producer prices were up 115.2% (NAHB analysis of BLS data).
  • Construction employers paid $51.96 per hour worked in June 2026, of which $36.13 was wages (BLS).
  • The median plumber, pipefitter or steamfitter earned $30.67 an hour in May 2025 (BLS).
  • Median Contracting revenue on Jobber rose 8% year over year in Q3 2026 (vendor data from its own customers).

What changed in contractor costs this fall?

The October producer price data, analyzed by NAHB on November 13, show two different problems. Materials keep climbing: building material prices rose 0.5% in the month and 5.8% over the year, with softwood veneer and plywood up 24.5% over the year. Fuel is the shock: No. 2 diesel fell 4.6% in October yet remained 115.2% above a year earlier. These are producer prices, so your pump receipt and supplier invoice are the numbers that belong in your estimate.

Selling prices in the wider industry moved by a similar order of magnitude. The BLS index for final demand construction, which tracks new nonresidential building work, was up 5.5% over the 12 months to October. That is not a residential service rate, so treat it as context, never as your price increase.

Owners describe the squeeze plainly in the NFIB report. A construction respondent in Maine wrote that fuel prices were becoming so high that "we will have to start adding fuel surcharges when billing," for the first time in almost 28 years. One in Michigan said it is "hard to keep passing the entire cost on to our customers."

How much does one billable hour really cost you?

A billable hour is an hour a customer pays for, including travel if you charge it. Shop time, callbacks, estimates, supply runs and paid leave are not. Your rate has to carry all of them.

Start with wages. The BLS median for plumbers, pipefitters and steamfitters was $30.67 an hour in May 2025. Then add the burden. In the June 2026 Employer Costs for Employee Compensation release, private construction employers spent $51.96 per hour worked, including $36.13 in wages and $15.84 in benefits and legally required costs (figures are rounded). The ratio is 1.438. Applying it to the plumber median gives $44.11 per hour worked.

That ratio is an industry average, so it is a stand-in. A shop with no health plan will sit lower, and one with high workers' comp rates may sit higher. Pull your own from payroll reports.

The rest of the example uses assumed figures that you should overwrite: three field techs, 2,000 worked hours each, 65% of those hours billable, and $150,000 a year in overhead (office pay, rent, trucks, insurance, software, marketing and the owner's non-field salary).

StepMathResult
Billable hours3 x 2,000 x 65%3,900
Labor per billable hour$44.11 / 0.65$67.86
Overhead per billable hour$150,000 / 3,900$38.46
Cost per billable hour$67.86 + $38.46$106.32
Rate at 15% net margin$106.32 / 0.85$125.08

One trap costs $2.81 an hour here. Marking cost up by 15% gives $122.27, which is a 13% margin. For a true 15% margin, divide by 0.85. On 3,900 hours the gap is about $11,000 a year.

Why do billable hours matter more than your hourly wage?

Change only the utilization figure and the answer swings hard:

Billable share of worked hoursCost per billable hourRate at 15% margin
55%$125.65$147.82
65%$106.32$125.08
75%$92.14$108.40

Ten points of utilization is worth roughly $17 to $23 an hour on the rate. Before copying a competitor's number, export 90 days of timesheets and invoices and divide billed hours by paid hours. A rival with tighter routing can charge less than you and earn more.

The cheapest billable hours are often the ones you already lost: calls nobody answered and estimates nobody chased. Run your own numbers in the missed call cost calculator and compare ways to follow up on estimates.

Should you raise the rate or add a fuel surcharge?

Size the problem first. A truck driving 15,000 miles a year at 12 miles per gallon burns 1,250 gallons, so every $1 per gallon costs $1,250 a year. Spread over that tech's 1,300 billable hours, it is $0.96 an hour. Both mileage and fuel economy are examples.

For most residential service shops, we would put fuel in the trip charge and review it quarterly: one number to explain, tied to the cost that moved. A separate surcharge makes sense when fuel is a large, volatile share of the job, as with heavy equipment or long rural routes. In that case, write down the trigger price, the amount, and the condition under which it disappears. Disclose it before the customer agrees. State fee-disclosure rules differ, so check yours before adding a line item.

How many jobs can you lose after a price increase?

Contribution margin is the share of the price left after costs that vanish if the job does not happen, such as parts, subcontractors and job fuel. With contribution margin m and price increase p, the volume you can lose while keeping the same contribution dollars is p / (m + p).

Contribution marginRaise 3%Raise 5%Raise 10%
25%10.7%16.7%28.6%
40%7.0%11.1%20.0%
55%5.2%8.3%15.4%

Discounts run the other way, and faster. At a 40% contribution margin, a 5% discount needs 14.3% more jobs to earn the same contribution. At 25%, it needs 25% more.

The formula assumes similar job sizes and the same payroll whether or not a job happens. If losing work lets you cut paid hours, the real threshold is looser; if it costs you repeat customers or referrals, it is tighter. A shop with a thin backlog should test an increase on new estimates in one service line before rolling it out.

What is the market telling you right now?

Demand is uneven but not collapsing. Jobber's Q3 2026 report, published in November, says median Contracting revenue (electricians, HVAC, plumbers and other trades) rose 8% year over year, building to 13% in September, and that Contracting was the only segment scheduling more new work than a year earlier. Jobber sells software to these businesses and measures a cohort of its own users since January 2023, so struggling shops that left the platform may be missing.

The NAHB Remodeling Market Index read 62 in Q3 (above 50 means more remodelers rate conditions good than poor), with the lead component at 53. NAHB also reported that economic uncertainty is making some customers hesitate. The practical reading: raise prices where your schedule is full, and tighten scope and options where leads are soft.

Offer two or three scoped options instead of a discount. A smaller scope lowers the ticket while your hourly math stays intact. A script you can copy:

"Our rate went up on [date] because fuel and materials did. Here are two ways to do this job: option A covers [full scope] at $[price]; option B covers [reduced scope] at $[price] and leaves [item] for later. Both are good for 30 days."

What should you do before December ends?

Pull 90 days of billed and paid hours. Replace the burden ratio, overhead and utilization with your own. Put an expiration date on every open estimate, and apply the new rate to new quotes only. Then track close rate by service line against the loss threshold in the table. A handful of quotes proves nothing, so wait for a few dozen before judging.

BLS is scheduled to release September 2026 compensation data on December 16. It will also be the last stretch before a change in that series: starting with December 2026 data, BLS says it will remove workers' compensation costs from ECEC, so a burden ratio taken from later releases will need your comp premium added back.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my hourly rate as a contractor?

Divide loaded labor cost by your billable share, add overhead per billable hour, then divide by 1 minus your target margin. In our example, $106.32 of cost at a 15% margin gives $125.08.

What is the difference between markup and margin for contractors?

Markup is added on top of cost, while margin is the share of the price you keep. A 15% markup on $106.32 gives $122.27, which is only a 13% margin.

How many customers can I lose if I raise my prices 5%?

Divide the increase by the sum of your contribution margin and the increase. At a 40% contribution margin, a 5% raise holds contribution dollars unless you lose more than 11.1% of jobs.

Should I add a fuel surcharge in 2026?

Calculate the cost first: at 15,000 miles and 12 mpg, each $1 per gallon costs $1,250 per truck per year. Many service shops can build that into the trip charge; a separate surcharge needs a written trigger, upfront disclosure and a check of state rules.
